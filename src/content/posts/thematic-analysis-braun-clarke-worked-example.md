Thematic analysis is a method for finding, developing and interpreting patterns of meaning across qualitative data such as interviews or open survey answers.

Virginia Braun and Victoria Clarke set out six phases in 2006 and have since refined the approach into what they call reflexive thematic analysis. While working on dissertations, most student problems come from one confusion: treating themes as topic summaries. ## Why Do Students Struggle With Thematic Analysis?

Time is the honest answer. Plan for it. Ten one-hour interviews can easily take two to three weeks of steady analysis alongside other modules. Set realistic milestones. Allow enough time to revisit your coding framework and draft chapters. This preserves analytical depth and keeps you in full control of your interpretations.

## What Are The Six Phases Of Thematic Analysis?

Braun and Clarke first described the phases in their 2006 paper "Using thematic analysis in psychology" in *Qualitative Research in Psychology*. Their later work, including *Thematic Analysis: A Practical Guide* (2022), updated the wording. Use the version your module recommends, but understand what each phase does.

### Phase 1: Familiarisation

Read and reread the whole dataset. Keep a simple reflexive journal during this stage. If you transcribed the interviews yourself, you have already started. Make brief notes on anything that strikes you. Write down your first impressions, your assumptions about the participants, and any emotional reactions to the interviews. This makes your personal stance visible before you start formal coding. In practice, budget at least as long as the recordings run, and often more. Skipping this phase produces shallow codes.

### Phase 2: Coding

Work systematically through the data and attach short labels to anything relevant to your research question. A code can be semantic (close to what the participant said) or latent (an interpretation of what sits beneath it). Code the whole dataset, not just the vivid quotes. Do not stop at surface descriptions.

For example, *if a participant says they check a document three times before handing it in, a semantic code is "repeated checking of work". A latent code for the same line might be "fear of public exposure" or "managing institutional vulnerability".*

Aim to capture both across your transcripts.

### Phase 3: Generating Initial Themes

Cluster codes that seem to share a core idea. Group your related codes together using notes or visual maps. Ask yourself what ties these specific codes together. If five codes describe feelings of stress, do not just label them "stress". Look for the shared mechanism. For instance, whether that stress comes from unrealistic targets or a lack of peer support. At this stage, themes are candidates, not conclusions.

### Phase 4: Developing And Reviewing Themes

Check each candidate against the coded extracts and then against the whole dataset. Some themes will merge, split or disappear. This phase is where your analysis gets stronger. Review your candidates at two levels:

1.  Check each theme against all the data extracts you coded for it. This ensures that the quotes actually support the theme's core idea.

2.  Check the entire candidate thematic map against the full dataset. It confirms that the map tells a coherent, honest story that answers your research question.

### Phase 5: Refining, Defining And Naming Themes

Write a short definition for each theme stating its central organising concept. If you cannot define a theme in two sentences, it is probably two themes or none. Give each theme an informative, active name. Avoid one-word titles or broad topic labels such as "Communication" or "Supervision". Use names that convey an argument or finding, such as "Supervision as Surveillance Rather Than Support".

### Phase 6: Writing Up

The write-up is part of the analysis. Choose extracts that show the theme and then interpret them. A quotation followed by a paraphrase of the quotation adds nothing. Structure your write-up paragraphs systematically:

1.  Introduce the theme or specific sub-point with an analytical claim.

2.  Present a short, vivid data extract.

3.  Interpret the latent meaning of the extract, explaining what it reveals about the participant's reality.

4.  Connect this interpretation back to your central research question or existing literature.

## What Is The Difference Between Codes And Themes?

This code vs themes distinction is the one marking criteria turn on.

- A **code** captures a single idea in a small piece of data. "Feeling watched by senior staff" is a code.

- A **theme** is a pattern of shared meaning across many codes and participants, built around a central concept that says something about your research question. "Competence as a public performance" could be a theme.

- A **topic summary** merely aggregates participant remarks on a subject. Labelling a section "Experiences of supervision" indicates where to look rather than conveying an analytical finding, making it a topic rather than a true theme.

As a simple test, theme names that could be established prior to data collection generally signal a descriptive topic summary.

## How Does Coding Work In Practice? A Worked Thematic Analysis Example

Here are eight lines from an invented interview with a newly qualified nurse, written for illustration only.

<ol class="transcript">
<li><p>"On my first week I kept double-checking every drug
chart."</p></li>
<li><p>"Not because I didn't know it, but because someone might be
looking."</p></li>
<li><p>"The sister would come round, and you'd feel her reading over
your shoulder."</p></li>
<li><p>"I'd rather take twenty minutes than be the one who gets it
wrong."</p></li>
<li><p>"When I asked a question, I'd phrase it like I already knew the
answer."</p></li>
<li><p>"By month three I stopped doing that. A healthcare assistant
laughed at me."</p></li>
<li><p>"She said everyone checks, but nobody does it like a
performance."</p></li>
<li><p>"Now I check because I want to, not because of who's on
shift."</p></li>
</ol>

### Codes:

- Lines 1 and 4: over-checking to avoid error (semantic: time-consuming chart verification)

- Lines 2 and 3: feeling observed (latent: surveillance anxiety)

- Line 5: masking uncertainty when asking questions (latent: impression management)

- Lines 6 and 7: peer challenge from support staff (semantic: feedback from colleague)

- Line 8: internalised professional standard (latent: shift from external compliance to personal accountability)

### The Step Between Coding and Themes:

At first, it is tempting to create a candidate theme called "Ward Anxiety" by gathering lines 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 together. However, "Ward Anxiety" is simply a descriptive bucket. It does not explain *how* that anxiety operates or *why* the nurse responds that way. A closer examination of the codes reveals a broader pattern beyond basic nervousness: the intentional staging of safe practice for senior colleagues. This insight provides a clearer, interpretative theme.

### Two Candidate Themes:

- **Competence as performance.** Built from "feeling observed", "over-checking to avoid error" and "masking uncertainty". The central idea: early on, safety behaviours are partly aimed at an audience.

- **From performed to owned practice.** Built from "peer challenge" and "internalised standard". The central idea: an informal moment reframes the behaviour, and the reason for checking moves from audience to self.

In a real study, you would test both themes against every other transcript before keeping them. Notably, avoiding simple topic labels like "drug checking" keeps the analysis focused on underlying concepts.

### Writing Up The Theme: A Comparison

**Weak Write-Up (Paraphrase Only):**

*Participant A explained that by their third month, a healthcare assistant laughed at how they checked charts. The assistant noted that everyone checks charts, but not as a performance. After this conversation, Participant A changed their behaviour and now checks drug charts for their own reasons rather than because of who is on duty.*

**Strong Write-Up (Interpretive Analysis):**

*Clinical safety initially functioned as impression management, but informal peer interactions helped the participant re-anchor these practices within their personal professional identity. When challenged by a healthcare assistant about treating routine checks "like a performance" **(lines 6–7)**, the performative front collapsed. Rather than reacting defensively, the participant used this casual ward interaction to separate genuine patient safety from the anxiety of being judged: "Now I check because I want to, not because of who's on shift" **(line 8)**. The clinical protocol remained unchanged, but the motive shifted from institutional self-defence to internalised professional responsibility.*

## Reflexive Or Codebook: Which Approach Did You Use?

Braun and Clarke distinguish three broad families:

- **Reflexive thematic analysis**, where the researcher's interpretation is a resource and coding is flexible and organic. There is no fixed codebook and no inter-rater agreement score. This approach sits within an interpretivist or constructionist paradigm. Knowledge is viewed as being generated through interaction between the researcher and the data, as opposed to being discovered as an objective truth.

- **Codebook approaches**, such as framework analysis, which use a structured coding frame often agreed in advance. These are common in applied policy or health services research where multiple researchers need to code large datasets systematically.

- **Coding reliability approaches**, which aim for agreement between coders and often report statistics such as Cohen's kappa. These sit within a post-positivist paradigm that treats themes as pre-existing entities waiting to be measured objectively.

Say which one you used and keep your method consistent with it. A common error is to **claim reflexive thematic analysis and then report a kappa score.** If your supervisor or a second marker reviews your coding in a reflexive study, they should do so as a "critical friend" to discuss differing interpretations.

Another is to claim **"data saturation" in a reflexive study.** Braun and Clarke have argued that saturation does not fit the reflexive approach, so justify sample size through information power or the richness of your data instead. This suggests that the more relevant information your sample holds regarding your specific research question, the fewer participants you require. The condition is that the research question is based on clear study aims, specific sample characteristics, strong interview dialogues, and a focused analytical strategy.

## How Should You Write The Analysis Chapter?

Your analysis chapter should guide the reader through an interpretive narrative rather than simply presenting a catalogue of quotes. To structure the chapter clearly, work through these core elements:

- **Open with a brief map of your themes and how they relate.** Provide a short introductory narrative or an overview diagram outlining your candidate themes and their sub-themes. State clearly how the whole structure addresses your overarching research question.

- **For each theme, give a definition, two or three well-chosen extracts, and your interpretation of each**. Treat each theme as its own sub-heading. Begin with your two-sentence definition stating the central organising concept. Use data extracts purposefully. Never drop a long block quote into the text without actively pulling apart the participant's specific word choices, tone, or underlying assumptions.

- **Show variation: who did not fit the pattern, and why that matters.** Reflexive analysis is not about claiming 100% uniformity. Highlight disconfirming cases, outliers, or participants who resisted the broader trend. It demonstrates analytical integrity and shows the marker that you did not cherry-pick your data.

- **Link back to your research question and the literature, usually in the discussion chapter.** In your findings chapter, keep the primary focus on the participants' voices and your direct interpretation. Where appropriate, signpost how a pattern challenges or supports existing theoretical concepts. Save the extensive theoretical critique for your discussion chapter.

- **Keep an audit trail in an appendix: a sample coded transcript, a code list and an early thematic map.** This provides direct transparency for your examiners. It demonstrates that your final themes developed organically through systematic familiarisation and coding rather than preconceived assumptions.

**A useful rule of thumb for balancing your text:** ensure your own analytical commentary takes up at least as much page space as the quoted extracts. If an extract runs for four lines, your deconstruction of its latent meaning, institutional context, and analytical significance should span at least four to five lines.

## Where Does Thematic Analysis Usually Go Wrong?

- **Themes that are topic summaries.** If your theme is called "Financial Problems" or "Lack of Time", you have only named a topic from the interview. A theme must say something about the data. Ask yourself: what does the data show about this topic? For example, instead of "Lack of Time", a better theme is: "Using a Lack of Time to Justify Breaking Rules".

- **Saturation claimed without evidence.** . Do not say you stopped interviews because "no new themes appeared". In this method, themes come from your interpretation. Explain that your sample was big enough because your interviews were detailed, your questions were clear, and your participants gave deep answers.

- **No audit trail.** Markers want to see how your thinking developed over time. Save your work regularly with dates. Keep your first list of codes, your rough theme sketches, and your personal notes. Add a page or two of these working drafts in your appendix so the marker can see your progress step by step.

- **Quotes doing all the work.** Do not just paste quotes one after another with phrases like "another student said the same thing". The marker wants to read your thoughts. *Fix by adding at least as much interpretation as quotation.* After every quote, write three or four sentences explaining what the words mean, why the participant said them, and how they help answer your main research question.

- **Mixed methodological language.** Do not mix different research styles. Fix by choosing reflexive, codebook or coding reliability and using its vocabulary throughout. If you use Braun and Clarke's reflexive approach, do not talk about "staying completely objective", "avoiding bias", or "checking if two people agreed on the codes". In reflexive analysis, your personal view is a useful tool, not an error to remove.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How Many Interviews Do I Need For Thematic Analysis?

There is no fixed number. Student projects often use between six and fifteen interviews, but the right number depends on your question, the depth of each interview and how varied your participants are. Justify your sample in terms that fit your approach.

### Can I Use Software Such As NVivo For Thematic Analysis?

Yes. Software helps you organise codes and extracts, but it does not do the interpretation. Braun and Clarke's phases apply whether you code on paper or on screen.

### Is Thematic Analysis Qualitative Or Quantitative?

Qualitative. Counting how often a code appears can describe your data, but frequency alone does not make something a theme.

### What Is The Difference Between Inductive And Deductive Coding?

Inductive coding starts from the data. Deductive coding starts from existing theory or a set of concepts. Many projects combine both, and you should say which you used.
